Small Kitchen Before and After on a $200 Budget

This small kitchen before and after on a $200 budget is the honest story of one real room we fixed in a single weekend — a small kitchen makeover with no renovation and no drilling. Our kitchen was the room we avoided — small, dark, and always messy, even right after we cleaned it. There was no counter space to cook, no place to put groceries away, and the cabinets were so full that we kept the most-used pans on the stove. We are renters, so we could not tear anything out or drill into the walls, and our budget was $200. No renovation, no new appliances, just renter-friendly fixes that anyone can copy. What Was Wrong With the Kitchen Before?

The kitchen is about 70 square feet, with one short stretch of counter between the sink and the stove, a single overhead cabinet, and a fridge that takes up most of one wall. Before the makeover, the counter held a coffee maker, a toaster, a knife block, a dish rack, a fruit bowl, and a pile of mail.

That left about 12 inches of free counter, which meant we chopped vegetables on a cutting board balanced over the sink. The upper cabinet was painted a dull cream color that had yellowed, and the cabinet knobs were mismatched. The walls were a flat off-white, and the only light was a single ceiling fixture that made everything look gray.

Storage was the bigger problem. Pots and lids were stacked in a deep lower cabinet, so we had to pull everything out to reach the pan at the bottom. Spices were in three different places. Cleaning supplies lived under the sink in a jumble, and there was no place to hang dish towels except over the oven handle. The floor was fine, and the appliances worked, but the room felt cramped and tired. We did not need more space. We needed the space we had to work better.

How Did We Plan This Small Kitchen Before and After on a $200 Budget?

We set three rules before we bought anything: everything had to be removable without damage, nothing could require drilling or painting, and every change had to solve a real daily frustration. We spent one evening measuring the counter, the cabinet doors, and the empty wall beside the fridge, then made a list in order of impact.

We set a budget of $200 and kept a running total on our phone as we shopped, buying most things at Target, Walmart, and Amazon.

The plan was simple: clear the counter, add light and color with removable materials, give every group of items a home, and use the empty wall and the inside of cabinet doors. We used what we already had where we could, like mason jars for storage and a small plant we moved from the living room. Change 1: How Did Clearing the Counter Create a Landing Zone?

The first change cost nothing and made the biggest difference, because it gave us back about 30 inches of clear counter to cook on. We took everything off the counter, sorted it into daily, weekly, and rarely used, and kept only the coffee maker out. The toaster moved into a cabinet and comes out only on weekends, the knife block moved to a magnetic strip on the side of the fridge, and the fruit bowl moved to the top of the fridge.

We added a narrow dish drying mat, $18, instead of the large dish rack that took up a third of the counter. It rolls up when not in use and drains into the sink. We also added a small cutting board that fits over the sink, $28, which gives us an extra work surface when we need it. The mail now goes in a small basket by the front door, not in the kitchen. Cost for this change: $46.

Change 2: How Did Wallpaper and New Knobs Add Color to This Small Kitchen Makeover?

The kitchen needed color and light, but we could not paint, so we used peel-and-stick removable wallpaper — one of our favorite small apartment decor ideas for renters — on the backsplash area for $32 and matching matte black knobs for $24. The wallpaper took about an hour to apply in a soft sage green, wipes clean with a damp cloth, and instantly made the cream cabinets look fresh. We chose a light pattern on purpose, because a dark or busy pattern would have made the small room feel smaller.

We also swapped the mismatched cabinet knobs for matching matte black knobs, $24 for a pack of 10. We kept the old knobs in a labeled bag so we can put them back when we move out. The new knobs took 20 minutes to change with a screwdriver, and they make the cabinets look intentional instead of worn out. Together, the wallpaper and knobs cost $56, and they are the change guests notice first.

Change 3: How Did We Fix the Inside of the Cabinets and Under the Sink?

Next we fixed the inside of the cabinets, where the daily frustration lived, with a two-tier sliding organizer for pots, a lid organizer on the cabinet door, and a tension rod and caddy under the sink. The sliding organizer cost $38 and ended the daily digging, because pots are no longer stacked on top of each other. In the lower cabinet, the top tier slides out, and the bottom tier holds lids upright.

We also added a small lid organizer on the inside of the cabinet door with adhesive hooks, $12, which keeps lids visible and easy to grab. Under the sink, we added a small tension rod, $9, to hang spray bottles by their triggers, and a narrow caddy, $16, for sponges and dish soap. That freed up the floor of the cabinet for a small trash bin and extra paper towels. In the upper cabinet, we grouped spices in two small turntables, $14 for a set of two, so nothing gets lost at the back. We labeled the jars we already had instead of buying new containers. Cost for this change: $89, and it was worth every dollar because it ended the daily digging and stacking.

Change 4: How Did We Use the Empty Wall and Add Warm Light?

The last change used the empty wall beside the fridge, with three adhesive hooks, a narrow floating shelf, and a battery LED light strip. The hooks hold towels, an apron, and a shopping bag, the shelf holds a plant, a cookbook, and wooden spoons, and the light strip brightens the counter for evening cooking. The wall had been blank for a year, and nothing on it is drilled, so the shelf holds only light items to stay secure.

We added three adhesive hooks, $11, for dish towels, an apron, and a reusable shopping bag, and a narrow floating shelf with adhesive brackets, $29, for a small plant, a cookbook, and a jar of wooden spoons. We also added a battery-operated LED light strip under the upper cabinet, $19, with a remote control. It plugs into nothing and sticks on with adhesive, and it makes the counter much brighter for evening cooking. The warm light makes the sage wallpaper glow and the whole room feel cozy instead of gray. We kept the original ceiling light as it was. Cost for this change: $59. The four changes add up to $250 at full price, so we waited for a sale on the sliding organizer and used a $25 gift card we already had, bringing our out-of-pocket total to $195 — see the table below.

What Does the Kitchen Feel Like After a Small Kitchen Makeover?

The kitchen is the same size, but it feels completely different, because we now have clear counter space to cook, we can find spices and pans in seconds, and the room feels brighter and warmer. Dishes get washed and put away faster because the drying mat rolls up and the cabinets have a place for everything.

The best test was the first week after the makeover: we cooked at home five nights instead of ordering takeout, because cooking was no longer a chore.

The sage backsplash and black knobs make the old cabinets look cared for, and the plant and cookbook on the shelf make the room feel like ours. The room stays tidy with about five minutes of cleanup after dinner, because everything has a home and the counter starts clear. If we move, we can peel off the wallpaper, swap the knobs back, and take the organizers, hooks, and light strip with us. That is the point of a renter-friendly makeover: it makes daily life better now, without risking your deposit later.

Frequently Asked Questions

Can I do this makeover if I rent?

Yes. Every change in this makeover is removable. The wallpaper peels off, the knobs swap back, the hooks and light strip use adhesive, and the organizers lift out. Keep the original hardware in a labeled bag, take photos before you start, and check your lease for any rules about adhesive products. Test the wallpaper on a small hidden spot first.

What should I do first in a small kitchen?

Clear the counter first. Take everything off, keep only what you use daily, and find a home for the rest. That one step gives you more usable space than any product you can buy. After that, fix the cabinet that frustrates you most, usually the pots and pans cabinet or the space under the sink.

How long did this makeover take?

The whole project took one weekend, about six hours total including shopping. Clearing and sorting took about two hours, the wallpaper and knobs took about an hour and a half, the cabinet organizers took about an hour and a half, and the shelf, hooks, and light took about an hour. You can spread it over two weekends if you prefer, and do one change at a time.

How much does a small kitchen makeover really cost?

This one cost $250 at full price and $195 out of pocket after a $55 gift-card and sale saving, but the exact number depends on your kitchen. The counter and cabinet fixes cost $46 and $89, while the wallpaper, knobs, wall, and lighting cost $56 and $59. If you start with just the counter and cabinet changes, you can get the biggest daily improvement for about $135.

Can I copy this makeover in a kitchen with no overhead cabinet?

Yes — skip the under-cabinet light strip and the spice turntables, and put that money toward a narrow shelf or a rolling cart instead. The counter clearing, the removable wallpaper, the new knobs, and the under-sink organizer all work with or without an overhead cabinet. Measure your wall space first, and choose a shelf or cart that fits the gap beside your fridge.
